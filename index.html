Abstract
In the era of burgeoning research publications, the task of replicating and conﬁrming the
ﬁndings presented in research papers is becoming increasingly challenging. This report
outlines the methodological approach and the formidable challenges encountered while at-
tempting to reproduce the results of the paper titled ”Learning to Generate Reviews and
Discovering Sentiment”. This endeavor also extends beyond mere replication, as we strive to
validate the original paper’s claims on 4 new, unseen datasets, and test the model’s accuracy
in classifying the sentiment of these datasets.
1 Introduction
Reproducible research has been a growing trend in the ﬁeld of machine learning in recent years. Replicat-
ing research helps in ﬁnding new outcomes and conﬁrming that the research result is true for new unseen
data as well. However, there exist various issues such as the requirement of computational resources on
large and complex data sets, inadequate access to source code, and outdated versions of libraries.
In this paper, we outline the diﬃculties and successes in reproducing the results of the ‘Learning to
generate reviews and discovering sentiments’ paper. Our main goal is to show the initial claim of paper
holds and we further replicate the ﬁndings on unseen data sets to show that the results are consistent.
2 Sentiments Analysis
Sentiment analysis is a subﬁeld within natural language processing and data mining that focuses on
interpreting results related to the sentiments expressed in textual data, it determines a body of text’s
underlying tone and categorizes it as positive, negative, or neutral. In sentiment analysis there exist
two processes. In the ﬁrst process (Training) we have to train our model to associate text inputs to
corresponding tags. The feature extractor transfers the text input into a feature vector and pairs of
feature vectors and tags (e.g. positive, negative, or neutral) are fed into the machine learning algorithm
to generate a model. In the second process (prediction), the feature extractor is used to transform unseen
text inputs into feature vectors. These feature vectors are then fed into the model, which generates
predicted tags (positive, negative, or neutral).
2.1 Justiﬁcation
There are many factors one must look into when looking to reproduce research. First and foremost, the
choice of paper to reproduce is an utmost crucial one. Ideally, the paper should be relatively recent as
new research and papers may have come out if the paper is outdated. Furthermore, the paper should also
be inﬂuential in its ﬁeld, as papers that had little to no impact in its ﬁeld are of lower value to reproduce.
Finally, the code for the work should be publicly available, and not entirely diﬃcult to reproduce due to
the time constraint of our project.
Learning to Generate Reviews and Discovering Sentiment[1] is inﬂuential paper, which had a total of 422
citations, with 40 of those being considered highly inﬂuential citations. Furthermore, when the paper was

2.2 Evaluation Framework
Evaluation of the eﬀectiveness of the sentiment analysis is normally performed by test accuracy, F1 score,
and ROC curve. The initial model proposed in the paper was trained on Amazon review data set and
the performance of the model was tested on a few famous text classiﬁcation datasets such as the MR,
CR SUBJ, and MPQA (MR and CR datasets are sentences extracted from Rotten Tomatoes, a movie
review website, Amazon product reviews, SUBJ’s subjectivity/objectivity detection and MPQA’s opinion
polarity)
3 Description Of Original Dataset
The dataset initially used to train the model was the 2014 Amazon product review dataset that was
introduced as a training corpus. This dataset contains over 82 million product reviews, from around 20
million unique users ranging from May 1996 to July 2014.
The original metadata included in the dataset were ”reviews and ratings”, ”item-to-item relationships”,
”timestamps”, ”helpfulness votes”, ”product image”, ”price”, ”category”, and ”salesRank”. For training
and testing the model, only the review and ratings were taken into consideration. Due to the large size of
the initial dataset, the authors split it into 1000 shards containing equal amounts of ratings and reviews
and used 1 shard for training and 1 shard for testing.

4 Replication of Original Work
Replicating the original model proposed in the paper would prove to be an immensely diﬃcult task. The
primary hurdle was the age of the code and dataset, as both were made public approximately ﬁve years
ago.
To achieve a successful replication, it was important to ensure that the code remained in the same state
as during the original experiments. This entailed maintaining the model architecture, hyperparameters,
and training procedures to faithfully reproduce the original work. Additionally, implementing the same
performance evaluation metrics used in the paper, including test accuracy, precision, and AUC-ROC
curves, was essential to validate the replication.

4.1 Process
The Github repository linked in the paper provided all the source code as well as the code to run the
arguments. However, our team faced plenty of issues in getting the code to run on our local machine.
Listed below is the process of discovering those issues, and how we ultimately overcame them.
1. The model included in the GitHub link to the original paper would not run in any of our test
machines. This is largely because the version of TensorFlow used in the code was version 1.15, a
version no longer supported.
2. Furthermore, while the code of the model to predict sentiment was available, the code to generate
reviews and text based on the sentiment neuron was not available. The paper also failed to mention
where the sentiment neuron could be found.
3. Some details about text generation weren’t explicitly explained in the paper, with crucial model
information occasionally tucked away in a single line. Furthermore, the absence of a clear metric
for evaluating the generated text limited our ability to assess this aspect. As a result, our focus
was narrowed down to replicating the sentiment analysis component of the model.
4. We could not get the original model up and running. However, we found a PyTorch port version
that demonstrated how to train the model from scratch. With that, we were determined to retrain
the model from scratch.
5. To match the developmental environment of the code provided in the PyTorch port version, we
decided to use Google Colaboratory to run the code and train the model. We used the ”train”
and ”test” data ﬁles provided by the PyTorch repository, which was not the same dataset as the
Amazon dataset, but a snippet from the Standford Sentiment Treebank dataset.
6. Training the model proved to be a challenge to many of our members, as Google Colaboratory only
provided limited computational units to free or non-paying members. With the free account, most
of our members could only reach 6 to 8 epochs while training the model, which we felt was not
suﬃcient.
7. Luckily, one of our team members had a paid Google Colaboratory account and was able to train
a full model with the speciﬁc dimensions as follows:
• 4096-dimensional mLSTM
• 64-dimensional embedding
• 256-dimensional output
• 87572736 number of parameters
• 30 epochs
8. While the code to train the model was provided, the code did not include the Logistic Regression
needed to predict labels in a dataset. To address this, we managed to build a sentiment classiﬁer.
9. From the last presentation, we felt that our own personally trained model did not achieve the
accuracy score, precision score, and ROC curve that we wanted.
10. As such, in an endeavor to further improve the accuracy score, precision score, and ROC curve of
the new data, we decided to give the original pre-train model another go.
11. After some modiﬁcation and metric changes, we managed to get the original included pre-train
model working on our new data.
12. New classiﬁcations showed an improved result in accuracy scores, precision scores, as well as the
ROC curve. These new results will be displayed in the ”Result on New Data” section of the paper.

5.1 Yelp Dataset, collected and processed by Phan Vinh Phu
The Yelp Dataset
2
, provided by Yelp’s businesses, is a dataset constructed by randomly taking 130,000
training samples and 10,000 testing samples across 8 metropolitan areas in the USA and Canada.
Yelp’s platform hosts diverse reviews spanning an array of establishments, including restaurants, salons,
clinics, and various others. The dataset comprises 40,000 rows with two main columns. The ’rating’
column enables users to assign ratings from 1 to 5 stars, while the ’review’ column contains the associated
text of the reviews.
To prepare the dataset for our model, we performed several transformations. First, we renamed the
’rating’ column to ’label’ to better align with our classiﬁcation task. Next, we converted the ratings,
originally ranging from 1 to 5 stars, into binary labels. Ratings of 1 or 2 stars were labeled as 0, signifying
a negative sentiment. We excluded 3-star ratings, considering them neutral. Finally, we labeled reviews
with 4 or 5 stars as 1, indicating a positive sentiment.
We viewed the diverse range of Yelp reviews as an intriguing challenge for our model. Additionally,
exploring how a model trained predominantly on product reviews could adapt to classifying location-
based reviews added an interesting dimension to our study.

Another potential reason why the accuracy is terrible could be potential issues with the labeling. Unlike

7 Reﬂections
The overall replication of the paper can be considered a success. When testing the results with our new
data set, the model was largely able to perform as well as it claimed in the paper. However, we did
encounter certain problems while replicating the original work. An example of the problems we faced
included not being able to run the code originally provided by the paper. As such, we had to use the
PyTorch version and build the classiﬁer from scratch. We eventually succeeded in reproducing the original
code and pertaining the model with new data.
As we investigated the paper more deeply, we encountered several issues that made this task troublesome.
Initially, when we faced with bugs and issues from the original code, the impatience and lack of consistency
led us to give up on it. However, we later realized that the key to success lay in patience and consistency.
It was only when we committed to thoroughly review and understand the code and make systematic
modiﬁcations that we achieved the desired results.
The best research work often leaves the reader with more questions than it answers. The performance of
the model on sentiment transfer seems to exhibit uncertainty dependent on the most recently modeled
samples from the unsupervised task. Due to this apparent dependence on data, we’d ideally like to
ﬁne-tune our model to a subset of data that is relevant to the task being transferred. With this in mind,
given a speciﬁc subset of data, how can we ﬁne-tune our language model to the speciﬁed data?
Sentiment analysis often classiﬁes sentiments into categories like positive, negative, or neutral, but in
practice, it encompasses a wide range of emotions and attitudes. Sentiments can be mixed, transitional,
or context-dependent, making them challenging to capture accurately. Sentiment analysis models have
evolved to recognize this complexity, aiming to provide a more nuanced understanding of text data.
Furthermore, while the ideas and features brought up in this paper were advanced for its time, the rise
of AI in recent years has made this paper somewhat obsolete. As such, it would be interesting to explore
this project with the tools and technology we now have.
